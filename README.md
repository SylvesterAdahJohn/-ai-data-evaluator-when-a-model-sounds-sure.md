# When a model sounds sure

Models rarely say "I'm not sure". A wrong answer arrives in the same calm voice as a right one, so tone tells you nothing. What I do instead is take the answer apart into claims and check each one at its source.

Below are three small examples. I wrote the model answers myself to show the habit. The sources are real and public.

---

## The wall and the Moon

**The question:** Can the Great Wall of China be seen from the Moon?

**The answer I'm checking:** "Yes, the Great Wall is visible from the Moon with the naked eye."

I went to NASA first, not to articles about NASA. NASA's own 2005 piece on this says the wall is not visible from the Moon, and that it is generally not visible to the unaided eye even from low Earth orbit. ([archived copy](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080917153200/http://www.nasa.gov/vision/space/workinginspace/great_wall.html))

**The reason I would write:**
> Fail. NASA states the wall is not visible from the Moon (NASA, 2005, archived link). The answer's main claim is wrong.

That is three sentences, it points at one page, and it says exactly what is wrong. A reviewer can confirm it without searching for anything.

---

## The proverb that isn't true

**The question:** Does lightning strike the same place twice?

**The answer I'm checking:** "No. Lightning never strikes the same place twice."

The National Weather Service lists this as a myth. Lightning often hits the same spot repeatedly, especially tall, pointed or isolated objects. ([NWS New York](https://www.weather.gov/okx/LightningSafetyAwarenessWeek))

Something small caught my eye while checking. The NWS New York office puts the Empire State Building at about 23 strikes a year, but another NWS office says nearly 100. ([NWS Riverton](https://www.weather.gov/riw/LightningSafety2020)) Two pages from the same agency gave two different numbers.

That does not change my verdict, because every figure is far above one strike. But if the question had asked for the exact yearly number, I would have flagged the disagreement and not quietly picked the figure I liked better.

**The reason I would write:**
> Fail. The National Weather Service lists this as a myth and says lightning commonly strikes the same place repeatedly (NWS New York). The main claim is wrong.

---

## The answer that is right

**The answer I'm checking:** "No. NASA says it cannot be seen from the Moon, and it is generally not visible to the naked eye from low Earth orbit either."

It matches the NASA page above, and it adds the orbit detail without overreaching.

**The reason I would write:**
> Pass. Both claims match NASA's 2005 statement (archived link). No errors found.

Not every review ends in a fail. A pass still deserves a reason, so the next person can see what it was checked against.

---

The habit I keep coming back to is simple. Take the claim apart, find each piece at its source, and write the reason so that someone else can verify it fast.
